Autoimmune disease is when your immune system mistakenly attacks self tissue. Modern medicine has provided many good treatments, but we still don't have a true solution that addresses the root cause of disease. Here, we'll talk about the history of autoimmune disease therapies that have led Immunospec to take the next step and provide a true solution to patients.

## The first drugs for autoimmunity

In 1901, [Paul Ehrlich first described autoimmunity as "Horror Autotoxicus"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rai2.12049) and by the 1950s, multiple distinct autoimmune diseases had been characterized. In the 50s and 60s, patients had limited options: corticosteroids, azathioprine and cyclophosphamide. These drugs helped patients control aggressive disease flare ups, but the drugs were short-acting and the side effects were considerable. Loss of immune defenses, severe toxicity, and kidney damage made it difficult to justify the benefits of the first-generation drugs unless the patient was dealing with serious, acute disease. To illustrate the point, cyclophosphamide is a [derivative of the infamous mustard gas](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09733698251355347) used in World War 1. Today, we only use first-generation drugs like azathioprine or cyclophosphamide for extreme life-threatening cases of autoimmune attack, cancer, and [organ transplant](https://www.aaaai.org/conditions-treatments/related-conditions/immunosuppressive) because newer drugs are designed to induce very specific effects without such widespread immune suppression. The corticosteroids used today are [variations of the original cortisone](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9780470015902.a0003627.pub2), which medicinal chemists have tweaked to reduce side effects, localize the effect of the medicine, and tune the half-life.

## The biologics revolution, immune pathway targeting, and cell therapy

Medicine never stops evolving, but when research breakthroughs and production methods suddenly make new things possible, an explosion of new drugs become available in a generational step-change. In 1998, [infliximab was approved by the FDA and became the first biologic drug for autoimmune disease](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6679695/). Drugs ending in -mab are monoclonal antibodies; they are designer antibodies that attach to one specific target. Infliximab grabs onto the TNF-α, a molecule that promotes inflammation, and removes it from circulation to help dampen inflammation in autoimmune diseases like rheumatoid arthritis and Crohn's disease. In the following decade, a whole portfolio of monoclonal antibodies would enter the clinic allowing doctors to target a number of different immune components. Monoclonal antibodies are also widely used in precision cancer treatment, as well as for antiviral treatment. While -mab drugs can accomplish some truly incredible things, they are hindered by their high cost to produce, and [unexpected immune phenomena](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8369271/). Monoclonal antibodies do not fix the underlying cause of autoimmune disease. They dampen immune responses with a degree of specificity, but the underlying autoimmune attack is still trying to target self-tissue.

In addition to the specificity brought by monoclonal antibodies, modern JAK inhibitors utilize traditional organic chemistry with modern knowledge of the inner workings of the immune system. Instead of targeting the entire immune system, JAK inhibitors selectively inhibit the Janus Kinases (JAKs) to dampen only one communication axis of the immune system, making them more precise than immunosuppressants. Despite the improvement over the last generation, JAK inhibitors still have side effects, they are rather costly, and patients may need [different JAK inhibitors for different disease states](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2024.1494901/full). To address the shared shortcomings of monoclonal antibodies and selective immune dampening, current efforts are focused on creating long-lasting and bio-similar or bio-identical treatments.

There is a growing movement to use cell therapy on autoimmune disease, including CAR-Treg technology, TolDC technology, and in-vivo peptide therapy. These approaches use cells as medicine, and they were not possible until recently. Many of these options involve high costs, but ongoing clinical trials [give us hope](https://autoimmune.org/blog/new-autoimmune-treatments/) that these methods will address underlying autoimmunity without the typical side effects of immunosuppression or immunomodulation.


## The next generation: antigen-specific tolerance

The first company to make a drug that truly addresses the root cause of autoimmune disease will win the market and free the patients. To do this, their therapy must precisely deal with the immune cells that have decided to attack healthy tissue in a cost-effective manner, with less toxicity than current options. Immunospec is pursuing antigen-specific tolerance because it satisfies all of the above criteria. By selectively removing the autoimmune attack without affecting the entire immune system, we can avoid immunosuppression.
